Neural Oscillation and Social Interaction

Problem: How does ethanol alter VTA-PFC neural coordination during social interaction?

Approach: Built end-to-end pipelines for PSD, coherence, PAC, and Granger causality on LFP signals.

Result: The analysis surfaced changes in theta-gamma coupling and cross-region interactions that were worth following more carefully.

My Role

  • Built end-to-end analysis pipelines from raw LFP to publication-ready figures
  • Integrated MATLAB + Brainstorm + Python workflows for seamless processing
  • Automated event-aligned processing for behavioral epochs
  • Implemented PSD, coherence, PAC, and Granger causality analyses
  • Discovered ethanol-specific effects on theta-gamma coupling during social approach

Methods & Tools

  • Signal processing: Bandpass filtering, Hilbert transform, time-frequency decomposition
  • Metrics: Power spectral density (PSD), coherence, phase-amplitude coupling (PAC), Granger causality
  • Tools: MATLAB, Brainstorm toolbox, Python (NumPy, SciPy), data visualization
  • Workflow: Event-aligned epoching, automated batch processing, reproducible pipelines

Key Findings

Integrated behavioral segmentation with neural data to examine directional connectivity and individual variability in affect-modulated circuit communication. Shared this work at Summer Symposium 2025 and Research Pathway 2026.

What made this work especially interesting was that integrating behavior with neural analyses required more than stacking methods together; I had to keep revising the pipeline so the metrics remained interpretable in the context of the experiment rather than drifting into abstraction.

Reproducibility

  • Data: Internal lab dataset (Melón Lab, Wesleyan University)
  • Code: Available upon request / private GitHub
  • Environment: MATLAB R2023b, Python 3.10, NumPy, SciPy

Next Steps & Reflection

This project taught me the value of building flexible, end-to-end pipelines that can handle complex experimental designs. The integration of multiple analysis methods (PSD, coherence, PAC, Granger causality) revealed patterns that no single metric could capture alone. I'm particularly interested in extending this work to understand how individual differences in circuit dynamics relate to behavioral variability.

Boolean Genetic Networks

Problem: How sensitive are genetic regulatory networks to mutations across different attractor states?

Approach: Simulated Kauffman Boolean networks with mutation sensitivity analysis on local machines.

Result: Explored attractor-dependent vulnerability patterns and the computational trade-offs involved in scaling the simulations.

My Role

  • Designed and implemented Boolean network simulations (Kauffman nets)
  • Developed mutation sensitivity analysis across different attractor states
  • Optimized memory usage and computational performance for larger local runs
  • Experimented with basic parallelization strategies on local hardware

Interactive Heatmap

Click “Simulate mutation cascade” to see how activity spreads across a small Boolean network toy example.

What I found compelling here was how quickly a simple model can become difficult to reason about at scale, which made computational efficiency and conceptual clarity feel like part of the same problem.

Methods & Tools

  • Model: Boolean genetic regulatory networks (Kauffman networks)
  • Analysis: Attractor identification, mutation sensitivity, robustness measures
  • Optimization: Memory-efficient data structures and exploratory parallel processing on local machines
  • Tools: Python, NumPy, computational biology modeling

Key Findings

Different attractor states showed varying degrees of sensitivity to mutations, suggesting that network topology and current state jointly determine robustness. Some attractors were remarkably stable, while others were highly vulnerable to single-node perturbations. This has implications for understanding biological robustness and evolvability.

Reproducibility

  • Code: Python simulation framework with configurable network parameters
  • Environment: Python 3.10, NumPy, local development environment

Next Steps & Reflection

This project taught me the importance of computational efficiency in large-scale simulations, even when running on local machines. What started as a straightforward modeling question became an exercise in thinking about optimization and potential future scaling. I'm interested in extending this approach to more realistic network topologies informed by empirical gene regulatory data, and exploring how network structure shapes evolutionary dynamics.

Learning My Own Rhythm

At Wesleyan, I started noticing that I rarely learn in a single lane for very long. My semesters have often included science, mathematics, art, music, and language work all at once, alongside jobs, mentoring, and projects that asked for different kinds of attention.

At first, I mostly experienced that range as pressure. I wanted to do everything well, and I tended to respond by tightening up. Over time, I have become more interested in a different question: not how to fit more in, but how to keep different commitments in conversation without flattening any of them.

That shift has changed how I think about busyness. I still like full days, but I care more now about whether the pace has structure. When it does, moving between research, teaching, and creative work feels less like fragmentation and more like a way of keeping my mind awake.

I work best with some range in my life, as long as the range still has form. That has become one of the quiet conditions for doing my strongest work.

When the Workflow Starts to Hold

One part of research I have grown to trust in myself is the transition from mess to structure. I like taking a scattered workflow, figuring out what is fragile, and slowly turning it into something another person could read, rerun, and build on.

In neuroscience work, that often means moving between Brainstorm, MATLAB, Python, and R while keeping the underlying question intact. I work on filtering, epoching, PSD, coherence, phase-amplitude coupling, and Granger causality not as isolated techniques, but as parts of a pipeline that should remain interpretable from raw signal to final figure.

Presenting “Statistical Approaches for Neural Oscillation and Social Behavior Analysis in the Ventral Tegmental Area” at Exley Science Center, Wesleyan University, Middletown, CT

That instinct extends to machine learning too. I am less interested in models as black boxes than in the full loop from prototype to interpretation: what the model seems to be learning, how stable that pattern is, and how to show it clearly without overclaiming.

Design helps here more than people sometimes expect. I think of scientific graphics as information design problems, where alignment, white space, hierarchy, and color all affect whether a result becomes legible or stays muddy.

Teaching, Then Understanding More

Teaching has become one of the clearest ways I learn what I actually understand. If I cannot explain an idea simply, I usually take that as a sign that I need to think about it again.

As a Course Assistant for Deep Learning and Large Language Models: An Introduction, I learned that students often struggle less with individual steps than with the shape of the concept itself. That pushed me to explain ideas more visually and more patiently, especially when abstract material needed to feel tangible before it could feel rigorous.

In biology tutoring and international mentoring, I have become more aware that explanation is also a form of listening. Different students need different entry points, and finding the right one often matters as much as knowing the material yourself.

That has made teaching feel closely related to research for me. In both cases, the work is not only to have a good answer, but to make the path toward that answer clearer for someone else.

Staying Clear When Things Get Messy

Under pressure, I usually become quieter and more methodical. That has turned out to be useful in both lab and team settings.

When something breaks, my first instinct is usually to reduce noise: check the assumptions, isolate the moving parts, and keep the problem from becoming larger than it is. I have found that this steadiness often matters as much as the technical fix itself.

I care a lot about standards, but I try not to turn that into pressure for the people around me. I would rather contribute calm, clarity, and follow-through than intensity for its own sake.

For me, composure is not emotional distance. It is a way of keeping enough room in the situation to think well and act usefully.

Arts in the Flow

When I first came to Wesleyan, I signed up for Drawing I, even though I had never done anything close to serious drawing before. I thought drawing was mostly about skill, like getting every line right, fixing mistakes, and working until the picture looked "perfect." So when our big assignment was a life-size self-portrait, I threw myself into it.

I spent hours staring at my own reflection and the huge sheet of paper in front of me, trying to capture every line of my face. Sometimes I'd erase the same section five, six times, because it didn't look exactly right. I was working so hard that I didn't even realize how tense I was, how much I was fighting with the drawing. I thought that was what "working hard" meant: keep pushing, fix every problem, and eventually it will turn out the way you want.

One day, my professor came by, looked at what I was doing, and said softly, "It's not about how much time you spend or how many details you fix. It's about the flow of the whole drawing." That stopped me.

I stepped back and looked at the page differently. I started noticing how the lines connected, how the space felt on the paper. The drawing slowly became less stiff. It felt like it had air in it now, like it was alive.

Later, in my private jazz vocal lessons, my professor told me almost the same thing about singing: "When you improvise, stop trying to construct what you think will sound good. Let the music flow out naturally." The problem was that I became rigid and stiff whenever I tried to force a harmonious sound. At first, I didn't really understand it. I still wanted to control. I tried to plan the "right" notes. But the more I forced it, the more rigid it sounded. Only when I relaxed and trusted the music did it start to feel real and natural.

This shift made me realize that striving doesn't always mean being in control. I've always had a competitive streak, and I hate leaving things in a state of "not good enough." But now I'm starting to see that sometimes I have to loosen up first, give myself space to feel and experiment, instead of constantly chasing perfection.

When Art Sharpens Scientific Vision

For me, interdisciplinarity is less about collecting fields than about noticing where different ways of thinking sharpen each other.

In scientific research, I study neuroscience and computational models, analyzing the rhythms of brain signals and their connections to behavior. Scientific training has conditioned me to describe phenomena through logic, data, and causality; yet, I've gradually realized that science's language often falls short of fully expressing the complexities of life. Painting helps bridge this gap: in sketching, I must grasp how light and shadow sculpt space, and how forms extend beyond the paper's surface. This visual discipline sharpens my ability to discern structural relationships in scientific charts, teaching me how to perceive the "aesthetic of form" behind data.

Once, while designing a poster, I spent hours adjusting colors and layout to ensure viewers could grasp the flow of experimental logic within seconds. These approaches, perhaps not considered "traditional science," allowed me to discover a new sense of order in expression, an order that integrates different modes of thinking.

Philosophical studies similarly broadened my cross-disciplinary perspective. Reading Husserl, I was struck by how his descriptions of "structures of experience" mirrored neural representations. Studying art history revealed that formalist painters, like scientists, uncovered visual laws through experimentation. Scientific training equipped me with precision and validation skills, while art and philosophy taught me to remain open amidst uncertainty.

That exchange between precision and openness has become central to how I learn. I look for aesthetic order inside science and logical structure inside art, and I trust the conversation between them more than any rigid boundary.
